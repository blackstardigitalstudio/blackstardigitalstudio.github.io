Did Týr Once Rule the Norse Gods Before Odin?

Did you know that Týr, the one-handed god of law and heroic glory in Norse mythology, might have originally been the chief god of the Germanic pantheon, even more prominent than Odin? Early linguistic and archaeological evidence suggests he was once considered the principal deity.

While Odin is famously the Allfather in the Eddas, linguistic comparisons show Týr's name (Old Norse: Týr; Proto-Germanic: *Tīwaz) is a direct cognate with the names for the supreme sky-gods in other Indo-European mythologies, such as Zeus (Greek) and Dyaus Pita (Vedic Sanskrit), and Latin's Jupiter (from *Dyēus Ph₂tḗr). This strong etymological link suggests that a deity akin to Týr was once the paramount sky-father for the Proto-Germanic peoples. Over time, as cult practices and theological importance shifted, the aggressive, magical, and cunning Odin (Woden/Wotan) rose to prominence, eventually supplanting Týr's earlier role as the chief deity.
