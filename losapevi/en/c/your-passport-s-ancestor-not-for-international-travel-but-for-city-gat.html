Your Passport's Ancestor: Not for International Travel, But for City Gates!

The very first 'passports' weren't designed for international border crossings; they were often royal letters or documents granting safe passage through a specific territory or even just a city gate within one's own country.

In medieval Europe, a 'passport' (from French *passer* 'to pass' and *port* 'a port, gate') was essentially a plea from a monarch or local noble to other authorities to allow the bearer to travel unhindered and safely through their lands. These documents were crucial for merchants, pilgrims, or emissaries to avoid brigands and ensure hospitality, predating the modern system of nation-states and formalized border control by centuries. The idea of a passport as a national identification for international travel truly solidified only in the 20th century, particularly after World War I. Modern passports now contain advanced security features to verify identity and citizenship across international boundaries.
